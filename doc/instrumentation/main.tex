% TODO: add co-author.
\author{Mike Rainey}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
A parallel program that runs on $P$ cores is rarely $P$ times faster
than its sequential counterpart, and the first question is where the
rest of the time went.
%
A work-stealing scheduler can answer, if it records what each worker
is doing: running the program, scheduling, or looking for work.
%
From those three totals, a speedup factors into parts that each name
one cause of loss, and from a log of the same changes of state, a trace
viewer shows when each loss occurred.
%
The difficulty is cost.
%
A fork takes a few nanoseconds, about as long as reading a clock, so a
scheduler that reads the clock at each fork distorts what it measures.
%
In this short paper, we show that under native fork join, no fork needs
to be instrumented: every change of state happens in the scheduler
loop, which a fork enters only if its second branch is stolen.
%
So the instrumentation costs a few clock readings per steal, and
nothing per fork.
%
The same property lets a program measure any region of its execution,
such as an algorithm without the loading of its input: it subtracts
two snapshots of the workers' records, and no worker is stopped and
nothing is reset.
%
We describe what to record, where to record it, and what to avoid, and
we extend micro taskparts, a minimal implementation of native fork
join, accordingly.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{What instrumentation is for}

Suppose a fork-join program takes time $T_s$ when its forks are
replaced by ordinary calls, and time $T_P$ on $P$ workers under a
work-stealing scheduler~\cite{blumofele99}.
%
Its speedup, $T_s/T_P$, is usually less than $P$.
%
The causes of the loss are few, and they call for different remedies.
%
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The forks themselves cost time}, even when nothing is
  stolen.
  The remedy is to fork less often, by running small problems
  sequentially.
\item \emph{The same work takes longer on $P$ cores than on one.}
  The cores compete for memory bandwidth and for cache, some cores are
  slower than others, and many slow down when all are busy.
  This is \emph{work inflation}~\cite{acar2017inflation}.
  The remedy lies in the program and the machine, not in the
  scheduler.
\item \emph{Workers are idle}, because the program offers too little
  parallelism, or offers it too late.
  The remedy is in the algorithm.
\item \emph{Workers are scheduling}: stealing, allocating stacks, and
  switching contexts.
  The remedy is in the scheduler, or in forking less often.
\end{itemize}
%
A running time alone cannot tell these causes apart.
%
Acar et al.~\cite{acar2017inflation} show that a few totals, kept by
the scheduler, can: they factor a speedup into parts, one per cause.
%
Totals say how much time was lost, but not when.
%
For that, the scheduler keeps a log, which a trace viewer such as
Perfetto~\cite{perfetto} displays as one timeline per worker.

Both are worth having only if they are cheap, for two reasons.
%
Instrumentation that slows the program changes the quantity it
reports.
%
And instrumentation that must be compiled in separately tends to be
absent on the day it is needed.
%
The fork is where the danger lies.
%
Under native fork join~\cite{nativeforkjoin}, a fork whose second
branch is not stolen costs a few nanoseconds more than two calls,
which is about what one reading of a clock costs.
%
A program may fork tens of millions of times a second.

Our observation is that, under native fork join, the fork needs no
instrumentation at all.
%
A worker changes what it is doing only in its scheduler loop, and a
fork whose second branch is not stolen never enters the loop.
%
So everything above can be recorded in the loop, at a cost
proportional to the number of steals, which is far smaller than the
number of forks~\cite{blumofele99,arora98}.

A benchmark rarely wants these records for a whole process.
%
It wants them for the algorithm, without the loading of its input, and
usually several times over.
%
Here the same observation helps again.
%
A worker that finds nothing to steal changes nothing in its record, so
between two computations the records of all the workers stand still,
and the program can read them without stopping anyone.
%
A region of the program is then measured as the difference of two such
readings.

Section~\ref{sec:what} defines what is recorded and what it tells us,
Section~\ref{sec:where} shows where the scheduler records it,
Section~\ref{sec:region} shows how a program measures a region,
Section~\ref{sec:cost} discusses the cost, and
Section~\ref{sec:techniques} describes how to keep that cost from
leaking onto the fork.
%
The appendices cover how to collect a factored speedup, the trace
format, the clock, measurements, and the worker threads.
%
This paper builds on the algorithm and the notation of the native fork
join paper~\cite{nativeforkjoin}; Section~\ref{sec:where} recalls what
it needs.
%
An extended micro taskparts accompanies this paper.

\section{What is recorded, and what it tells us}
\label{sec:what}

\paragraph{States}
%
At every moment of a run, each worker is in exactly one of three
states.
%
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Working}: running client code, which includes the forks
  that client code makes.
\item \emph{Scheduling}: in its scheduler loop with something to do,
  such as counting a branch at a join, preparing a stolen task, or
  switching to one.
\item \emph{Idle}: in its scheduler loop, and failing to steal.
\end{itemize}
%
The scheduler keeps, for each worker, the total time spent in each
state.
%
Summed over the workers, these give the total work $W_P$, scheduling
time $S_P$, and idle time $I_P$ of a run on $P$ workers.
%
Because each worker is always in exactly one state,
\[ P \cdot T_P = W_P + S_P + I_P. \]

\paragraph{Factoring a speedup}
%
Let $W_1$ be the work of a run on one worker.
%
Then the speedup is, exactly,
\[
\frac{T_s}{T_P} \;=\; P \cdot \frac{T_s}{W_1} \cdot \frac{W_1}{W_P}
  \cdot \frac{W_P}{W_P + S_P + I_P}.
\]
%
Each of the three fractions is 1 in the ideal case, and each falls
below 1 for one of the causes of Section~1.
%
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $T_s/W_1$ is the price of the forks: how much slower the
  parallel code is than the sequential code, with no other worker
  around.
\item $W_1/W_P$ is the inverse of the work inflation: how much more
  time the same work takes when $P$ workers share the machine.
\item The last fraction is the \emph{utilization}: the share of the
  workers' time that goes to work, which idle time and scheduling time
  reduce separately.
\end{itemize}
%
This is the idea behind the factored speedup plots of Acar et
al.~\cite{acar2017inflation}, adapted to the three states above.
%
\paragraph{Factored speedup plots}
%
Plotted against the number of workers, the factors show at a glance
where a speedup goes (Figure~\ref{fig:factored}).
%
The dashed line is the ideal, $P$, and the black curve is the speedup
that was measured.
%
The space between them is divided into bands, one per cause of loss,
by the curves $P \cdot T_s/W_1$, which only the price of the forks
holds below the ideal, then $P \cdot T_s/W_P$, which work inflation
lowers further, then $P \cdot T_s/(W_P + I_P)$, which idle time lowers
further still.
%
What separates that last curve from the speedup is scheduling.
%
For the four programs of the figure, which sort, compute a convex
hull, and search a graph, nearly all of the loss is work inflation.
%
The scheduler's own share, the two bands at the bottom, is barely
visible, and so is the price of the forks, except for BFS.
%
Two of the programs bend at 5 workers, where the slower cores come
into use, and two lose ground from the second worker on, before any
slower core is involved.
%
A plain speedup curve would show the same black line for all of these
causes.

Computing a factored speedup takes two instrumented runs, on 1 and on
$P$ workers, and one run of the sequential program.
%
Appendix~\ref{sec:collect} gives the recipe: which builds to run, and
which of the values that the scheduler reports stands for each symbol.

\paragraph{Counts}
%
The scheduler also counts steals, which says how often work moved
between workers, and which bounds the stacks allocated and the
contexts switched~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
%
A count of forks is useful too: divided into the work, it gives the
grain of the program, the time between forks.
%
But it is the one item here that belongs to the fork, and
Section~\ref{sec:cost} treats it apart.

\paragraph{The log}
%
The log holds the same information before it is totaled.
%
Each worker appends an entry whenever it changes state, giving the
time and the new state, and whenever it steals, giving the time and
the victim.
%
From a log, a viewer draws each worker as a timeline of working,
scheduling, and idle intervals, with a mark at each steal.
%
Such a picture shows what totals hide: a sequential phase, in which
one worker works and the rest are idle; a burst of steals as a
computation spreads out; or a worker that the operating system
descheduled.
%
The totals take constant space per worker, and the log takes space
proportional to the number of steals.

\section{Where it is recorded}
\label{sec:where}

\paragraph{Native fork join, in brief}
%
Under native fork join~\cite{nativeforkjoin}, each worker runs a
scheduler loop on its thread's own stack, and client code on separate
task stacks.
%
\lstinline|fork2join($\fo$, $\ft$)| pushes a task for $\ft$ on the
worker's deque, calls $\fo$, and pops.
%
If the pop succeeds, it calls $\ft$ and returns, without ever leaving
client code.
%
If instead the task was stolen, it saves the parent's context in the
join and switches to the worker's loop.
%
The loop counts the branches that reach a join, resumes the parent
once both have, and otherwise steals, starting each stolen task on a
fresh stack.
%
A task that finishes on such a stack switches to the loop as well.

\begin{figure}
\hrule
\begin{lstlisting}[escapechar=@]
type state = working | scheduling | idle
type recorder = {cur : state, since : time,
  total : time[state], steals : int,
  log : list of (time, state or worker)}

mark(s : state) -> void {  // called in loop only
  r = me.rec
  if (r.cur == s) return
  now = clock()
  r.total[r.cur] += now - r.since
  r.cur = s; r.since = now
  append(r.log, (now, s)) }  // if logging

loop() -> void {  // on the worker's own stack
  while (!done) {
    j = me.reached; me.reached = null
    if (j != null) {  // a branch reached j
      if (fetch_and_add(j.pending, -1) == 1) {
        mark(working)@\added@
        swap(me.k, j.k)  // resume the parent
        mark(scheduling) }@\added@
      continue }
    t = steal()
    if (t == null) { mark(idle); continue }@\added@
    mark(scheduling); me.rec.steals++@\added@
    t.k = new_context(new_stack(), enter)
    me.starting = t
    mark(working)@\added@
    swap(me.k, t.k)
    mark(scheduling) } }@\added@
\end{lstlisting}
\hrule
\caption{The scheduler loop of native fork join, with the lines that
  instrumentation adds.
  \lstinline|fork2join| is unchanged.}
\label{fig:loop}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Every change of state is in the loop}
%
A worker starts working when its loop switches to a task stack: to a
stolen task, or to a parent whose join is complete.
%
It stops working when something switches back to its loop: a fork
whose second branch was stolen, or a task that has finished.
%
Either way, the next thing to run is the instruction that follows a
\lstinline|swap| in the loop.
%
And a worker is idle only while its loop fails to steal.
%
So the loop can do all the recording, as Figure~\ref{fig:loop} shows.
%
\lstinline|mark($s$)| records that the worker is now in state $s$: it
reads the clock, charges the time since the last change to the state
the worker was in, and, in a build that logs, appends an entry to the
worker's log.
%
It does nothing if the worker is already in state $s$, so a worker
that fails to steal many times in a row reads the clock once.

\lstinline|fork2join| does not appear in Figure~\ref{fig:loop},
because nothing in it changes.
%
The code that switches from client code to the loop does not change
either: the loop records the change of state once it is running again.
%
So the few instructions of the switch are charged to work.

\paragraph{What a steal costs in clock readings}
%
A steal causes at most six calls to \lstinline|mark| that read the
clock.
%
The thief stops being idle, starts the stolen task, and returns to its
loop when the task finishes; if it then resumes the parent, that is a
fourth.
%
The victim returns to its loop when it finds its task stolen, and then
either becomes idle or starts something else.
%
A fork whose second branch is not stolen causes none.

\section{Measuring a region}
\label{sec:region}

A benchmark seldom wants totals for a whole process.
%
A typical one loads or generates its input, and only then runs the
algorithm to be measured.
%
It may first run the algorithm a few times unmeasured, to warm up the
machine.
%
And it may then take several samples in one process.
%
So the totals and the log must be able to cover a \emph{region} of
the program's execution, and to do so again and again.

\paragraph{Every thread is always in a state}
%
The workers' threads start once, and run until the program exits.
%
The thread that calls \lstinline|fork2join| from outside any task is a
worker too.
%
For the duration of such an \emph{outermost call}, it runs its
scheduler loop, starting with the task that makes the call, and it
returns to the program when that task has finished.
%
Between outermost calls, it runs the program's sequential code, so it
is recorded as working, and the other workers, with nothing to steal,
are idle.
%
So the three states cover every worker at every moment of the process,
and $P \cdot T = W + S + I$ holds for any stretch of time $T$, not
only for a whole computation.
%
In particular, a region that alternates sequential code with parallel
loops is accounted for in full: its sequential code shows up as one
worker's work and the others' idle time.

\paragraph{Snapshots, not resets}
%
A recorder is never reset: its totals only grow.
%
A recorder also says which state its worker is in and since when, so
its totals as of any instant can be worked out from it, by adding the
time since the last change to the current state.
%
A \emph{snapshot} is the sum of these over the workers, at one instant
(Figure~\ref{fig:snapshot}).
%
A program measures a region by taking a snapshot before it and another
after it: the totals of the region are the difference, and its log is
the entries between the two instants.
%
Regions can follow one another or nest, and a region costs the rest of
the program nothing.

\begin{figure}
\hrule
\begin{lstlisting}
snapshot() -> snap {  // between outermost calls
  for (w in workers, w != me)
    while (w.rec.cur != idle) yield()
  now = clock(); s = snap{at = now}
  for (w in workers) {
    r = w.rec
    s.total += r.total
    s.total[r.cur] += now - r.since
    s.steals += r.steals }
  return s }

launch(root : task*) -> void {  // outermost call
  mark(scheduling)
  me.starting = root; loop()  // until root is done
  mark(working) }
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Reading a recorder without stopping its worker}
%
Only its owner writes a recorder, but a snapshot reads all of them.
%
That needs no lock, and no help from the workers, provided the
snapshot is taken where a benchmark wants it anyway: between outermost
calls, by the thread that makes them.
%
There, no task exists, so nothing can be stolen.
%
A worker that is idle only tries to steal, and
\lstinline|mark(idle)|, called by a worker that is already idle,
writes nothing.
%
So once a worker is idle, its recorder and its log stand still until
the next outermost call, which only the thread taking the snapshot can
make.
%
The snapshot therefore waits until every other worker shows itself
idle, and then reads.
%
The wait is short: when an outermost call returns, each worker is one
failed steal away from idle.
%
The state is the only field that is read while its owner may be
writing it, so it is the only atomic one, and \lstinline|mark| writes
it last, so that whoever sees a worker idle also sees the totals that
the worker has just brought up to date.

The price is that a snapshot cannot be taken inside a computation.
%
The alternative that we know, in which every worker is made to reset
its own recorder at the start of each region, is harder to get right
and costs more (Appendix~\ref{sec:pool}).

\paragraph{A harness}
%
A benchmark seldom calls \lstinline|snapshot| itself.
%
It hands its algorithm to a harness, which runs the program's setup,
optionally runs the algorithm unmeasured for a while, and then runs it
a number of times, each between two snapshots, and writes one record
per run (Appendix~\ref{sec:collect}).

\section{What it costs}
\label{sec:cost}

\paragraph{Per fork, nothing}
%
With totals and the log turned on, a fork whose second branch is not
stolen runs the same instructions as with both turned off.
%
We mean this literally.
%
In micro taskparts, the compiled code of a client function that forks
is the same in the three builds, apart from the addresses of other
functions and one constant, the size of a worker.
%
Section~\ref{sec:techniques} explains what it took to make this true.
%
The running times agree to within about 1\%, on both of the machines
we measured (Appendix~\ref{sec:perf}).

\paragraph{Per steal, a little}
%
Each steal adds a few readings of the clock, each a single
instruction (Appendix~\ref{sec:clock}), a few additions, and, with the
log, up to seven entries of 16 bytes.
%
This is small beside what a steal already costs: a stack taken from a
pool and four context switches~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.

\paragraph{Per region, and per outermost call}
%
A snapshot reads $P$ recorders, after a wait that is usually over
before it starts.
%
An outermost call adds two readings of the clock, on the calling
thread.
%
Neither is on the path of a fork.
% TODO: certify the cost of an outermost call that does nothing. An
% informal test gave about 50 ns.

\paragraph{The count of forks}
%
Counting forks is the exception: it puts one increment of a counter in
memory on every fork.
%
Done well, this is cheap, but it is not free, so it is a separate
option.
%
Done carelessly, it is the costliest thing in this paper.
%
Table~\ref{tbl:cost} in Appendix~\ref{sec:perf} compares three ways to
count: in a counter private to each worker, in one atomic counter that
all workers share, and in private counters packed side by side in one
array.
%
On one worker, all three are cheap.
%
On several, the last two make a program that does little but fork
far slower than it is with no counting at all, and slower than it is
on one worker: on 4 workers, it takes \perftime{atomic}{4}\,s and
\perftime{packed}{4}\,s instead of \perftime{plain}{4}\,s.
%
The shared counter fails for the obvious reason: every fork on every
worker writes to the same cache line.
%
The packed counters fail for the same reason, less obviously: no two
workers write to the same counter, but several counters share a line.

\paragraph{Pay as you go}
%
A build of micro taskparts chooses among four levels: nothing; the
totals and the count of steals; those plus the log; and those plus the
count of forks.
%
The first three run the same code at a fork, so the choice among them
is a matter of memory for the log, not of time.
%
Only the count of forks is a real decision.

\section{Keeping the cost off the fork}
\label{sec:techniques}

What Section~\ref{sec:where} adds is simple.
%
The care goes into four things that would otherwise put the cost back
on the fork, or on other workers.

\paragraph{No synchronization}
%
Each worker has its own recorder, and writes to no other.
%
Another thread reads a recorder only when it has seen the worker idle
between outermost calls, when the recorder stands still
(Section~\ref{sec:region}).
%
So no log is locked, and a single field of a recorder is atomic: the
state, through which the worker says that it is idle.
%
\lstinline|mark| writes that field once per change of state, in the
loop.
%
The price is that totals are not available while a computation runs.
%
\lstinline|mark| needs no lookup of the current worker either: only
the loop calls it, and a loop always runs on its own worker.

\paragraph{No false sharing}
%
A recorder that no other worker writes to can still be slowed by
other workers, if it shares a cache line with something they write.
%
Two things qualify.
%
One is another worker's recorder, as with the packed counters of
Section~\ref{sec:cost}.
%
The other is a deque: thieves write to the deque of their victim.
%
So each recorder starts and ends at a cache-line boundary, as does
each deque.

\paragraph{No change to the layout that the fork uses}
%
A fork reads and writes a few fields of its worker, among them the
ends of the deque.
%
If the recorder were placed before those fields, their offsets would
change, and with them the instructions of every fork.
%
So the recorder comes last in the worker, after the deque.

\paragraph{No cold code inlined into hot code}
%
\lstinline|fork2join| is inlined into the client function that calls
it, which is what makes a fork cheap.
%
But then whatever \lstinline|fork2join| inlines becomes part of that
function too.
%
In micro taskparts, \lstinline|fork2join| begins with a test: if the
scheduler is not running, start it.
%
The code that starts it runs once, but when the compiler inlines it,
the client function must save, on \emph{every} call, each callee-saved
register that this code uses.
%
The instrumentation made this worse, because the code that starts the
scheduler now also reads the time and converts it to seconds, which
uses floating-point registers.
%
The first instrumented build of micro taskparts was slower at forking
for this reason alone, with not one instruction added to the path of a
fork.
%
The fix is to forbid the compiler from inlining the code that starts
the scheduler.
%
That also made the build with no instrumentation faster, as the
native fork join paper reports~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.

\paragraph{Comparing builds}
%
A last caution concerns measurement, not design.
%
Adding code anywhere in a program moves the functions that follow it,
and where a hot function lands, relative to cache lines, can change
its running time by a few percent~\cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}.
%
That is as large as the differences we are looking for.
%
So the builds we compare align every function to a cache line.

\paragraph{Implementation}
%
Micro taskparts with instrumentation is in the directory
\path{doc/instrumentation/microtaskparts} of the TaskPaRTS
repository~\cite{taskparts}.
%
It adds about 400 lines of C++ to the micro taskparts of the native
fork join paper: the recorder, the snapshots, the writing of the trace,
and worker threads that persist (Appendix~\ref{sec:pool}).
%
The harness is in \path{doc/instrumentation/benchmark}.

\section{Collecting a factored speedup}
\label{sec:collect}

This appendix says how a client of micro taskparts obtains each
quantity of Section~\ref{sec:what}.

\paragraph{Snapshots}
%
The function \lstinline|take_snapshot| returns a snapshot
(Section~\ref{sec:region}).
%
Given two snapshots, \lstinline|stats_between| returns the record of
the region between them, with times in seconds:
%
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
field & meaning \\
\midrule
\lstinline|nb_workers| & $P$ \\
\lstinline|exectime| & $T_P$: wall-clock time of the region \\
\lstinline|work| & $W_P$: time working, summed over workers \\
\lstinline|sched| & $S_P$: time scheduling, likewise \\
\lstinline|idle| & $I_P$: time idle, likewise \\
\lstinline|nb_steals| & steals \\
\lstinline|nb_forks| & forks, if the build counts them \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
%
In a build without totals, only \lstinline|exectime| is filled in.
%
\lstinline|print_stats| writes a record as one line of JSON.

\paragraph{The harness}
%
A program with a setup phase calls the harness:
%
\begin{lstlisting}
benchmark(body, setup, teardown, reset)
\end{lstlisting}
%
\begin{sloppypar}
The harness calls \lstinline|setup|, then \lstinline|body| a number
of times, with \lstinline|reset| in between, and measures each call
of \lstinline|body| as a region of its own.
%
It writes the records as a JSON list.
%
Two environment variables, whose names begin with
\lstinline|TASKPARTS_BENCHMARK_|, control it: \lstinline|NUM_REPEAT|
is the number of regions, and \lstinline|WARMUP_SECS| a time for which
the harness first calls \lstinline|body| unmeasured.
%
Whatever the program does before it calls the harness, such as
generating its input, is outside every region.
%
The interface is that of the benchmark harness of
TaskPaRTS~\cite{taskparts}, so programs written for that harness
compile against this one unchanged.
\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Three runs}
%
A factored speedup on $P$ workers takes three runs of the program,
with two builds.
%
The environment variable \lstinline|TASKPARTS_NUM_WORKERS| sets the
number of workers.
%
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
build & workers & read & as \\
\midrule
sequential & & \lstinline|exectime| & $T_s$ \\
with totals & 1 & \lstinline|work| & $W_1$ \\
with totals & $P$ & \lstinline|exectime|, \lstinline|work|,
  & $T_P$, $W_P$, \\
 & & \lstinline|sched|, \lstinline|idle| & $S_P$, $I_P$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
%
A build with totals is one compiled with \lstinline|-DMTP_STATS|.
%
The sequential build is the program with its forks replaced by calls:
\lstinline|-DMTP_SERIAL| makes \lstinline|fork2join| two ordinary
calls, and a program written over ParlayLib uses ParlayLib's
sequential mode.
%
Either way there is no scheduler, and the record of a region holds its
time only.
%
The second run is needed even though the third reports its own work,
because work inflation compares the work on $P$ workers with the work
on one.

\paragraph{The factors}
%
From these seven numbers:
%
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
speedup & $T_s / T_P$ \\
price of the forks & $T_s / W_1$ \\
work inflation & $W_P / W_1$ \\
utilization & $W_P / (P \cdot T_P)$ \\
share lost to idle time & $I_P / (P \cdot T_P)$ \\
share lost to scheduling & $S_P / (P \cdot T_P)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
%
The speedup is $P$ times the price of the forks, times the
utilization, divided by the work inflation.
%
The utilization and the two shares add up to 1, because the three
totals of a region add up to $P \cdot T_P$.
%
Running the third build at each number of workers of interest gives
the curves of a factored speedup plot.

\paragraph{An example}
%
For \lstinline|fib(38)| on 8 workers, the runs of
Appendix~\ref{sec:perf} give $T_s = \perftime{serial}{1}$,
$W_1 = \perftotal{work}{1}$, $T_8 = \perftime{stats}{8}$,
$W_8 = \perftotal{work}{8}$, $S_8 = \perftotal{sched}{8}$, and
$I_8 = \perftotal{idle}{8}$, all in seconds.
%
The speedup is about 2.5, far from 8, and the factors say why.
%
The price of the forks is about 0.4: this program does nothing but
fork, so the parallel code is two and a half times slower than the
sequential code before any other worker joins in.
%
The work inflation is about 1.4: of the 8 workers, 4 run on slower
cores, so the same work takes more processor time.
%
On 4 workers, all on the faster cores, the work is
\perftotal{work}{4}\,s, an inflation of about 1.03.
%
The utilization is above 0.99: scheduling and idle time together come
to less than a millisecond, over \perftotal{steals}{8} steals.
%
Table~\ref{tbl:factors} gives the same numbers for four programs that
do real work.
%
So this run loses nothing to the scheduler, and its remedies are to
fork less often and to expect less of the slower cores.

\paragraph{Cautions}
%
$T_s$ must time the same region of the program as $T_P$ does, which
the harness ensures when the same program is built both ways.
%
A snapshot is taken between outermost calls, by the thread that makes
them, so a region begins and ends there.
%
Each of the three runs is one sample.
%
As with any timing, each should be repeated, and the factors computed
from medians.

\section{The trace}
\label{sec:trace}

\lstinline|write_trace| writes the part of the logs between two
snapshots as one file in the JSON trace-event format, which
Perfetto~\cite{perfetto} opens directly.
%
Like a snapshot, it is called between outermost calls.
%
Each worker becomes a thread of the trace, named by a metadata event.
%
Each stay in a state becomes a complete event (\lstinline|"ph": "X"|),
named \lstinline|work|, \lstinline|sched|, or \lstinline|idle|, with a
start time and a duration in microseconds.
%
Each steal becomes an instant event (\lstinline|"ph": "i"|) on the
thief's thread, which carries the victim as an argument.
%
For example:
%
\begin{lstlisting}[language={},mathescape=false,basicstyle=\footnotesize\ttfamily]
{"name": "work", "ph": "X", "pid": 0, "tid": 3,
 "ts": 2570.130, "dur": 402.641}
{"name": "steal", "ph": "i", "s": "t",
 "pid": 0, "tid": 3, "ts": 2972.771,
 "args": {"victim": 1}}
\end{lstlisting}
%
A log entry in memory is 16 bytes: the time, in ticks of the clock,
what happened, and, for a steal, the victim.
%
Intervals are formed, and ticks converted, only when the file is
written.

\section{The clock}
\label{sec:clock}

\lstinline|clock()| reads a counter of the processor directly.
%
On AArch64, it is the virtual counter, \lstinline|cntvct_el0|, and on
x86-64, the time-stamp counter, read with \lstinline|rdtsc|.
%
Each is one instruction, with no call into a library or the operating
system.
%
The scheduler keeps times in ticks of this counter, and never converts
them while workers run.
%
To convert them afterward, it needs the length of a tick, which it
measures rather than looks up: each snapshot reads both the counter
and the system's monotonic clock, and the differences between two
snapshots give the length of a tick over that region.

The counter can be coarse.
%
A stay in the scheduling state may last only a few ticks, so a single
interval can be badly rounded.
%
The totals are still sound: each reading of the clock ends one
interval and begins the next, so the rounding of one interval is
returned to its neighbor, and the three totals of a worker always sum
to the time the worker ran.

If the operating system deschedules a worker, the time until it runs
again is charged to the state it was in.
%
The log shows such gaps only indirectly, as an interval that is longer
than it should be.

\section{The worker threads, and two alternatives}
\label{sec:pool}

Section~\ref{sec:region} relies on worker threads that outlive an
outermost call.
%
This appendix describes them, and then two other ways to measure a
region that we know of, and why we set them aside.

\paragraph{The threads}
%
Micro taskparts starts the threads of all workers but one at the first
outermost call or the first snapshot, and stops them when the program
exits.
%
The number of workers is read from the environment before any thread
starts, because programs size their tables by it.
%
The remaining worker is the thread that makes the outermost calls.
%
For each call, that thread runs its scheduler loop, starting with the
root task on a task stack, until the root has finished.
%
An idle worker yields its processor between attempts to steal, whether
or not a computation is in progress.
%
So between computations, all workers but one spin.
%
We accept that here: putting idle workers to sleep without losing
speedup is a subject of its own.

\paragraph{The root leaves its stack before it is announced}
%
Under native fork join, a branch is counted at its join only after it
has left its stack~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
%
The root task needs the same care once threads persist.
%
The thread that made the outermost call returns to the program as soon
as it learns that the root has finished, and the next outermost call
reuses the root's stack.
%
But the root may finish on another worker, which is then still running
on that stack.
%
So a root that finishes switches to its worker's loop first, and the
loop announces it.

\paragraph{The stacks of stolen tasks persist too}
%
The native fork join paper keeps the stacks that stolen tasks
release, since mapping a stack at every steal is costly on many
workers~\cite{nativeforkjoin}: each worker keeps a few of its own, and
the workers share a pool of the rest.
%
There, all of them are unmapped at the end of each outermost call.
%
Here they live as long as the workers, and are unmapped when the
program exits, so a later region finds the stacks of an earlier one.

\paragraph{Alternative: one launch per region}
%
An earlier version of this micro taskparts started its threads at each
outermost call, and stopped them when the call returned.
%
Then nothing needs to be reset, because every recorder is new, and
nothing is read while a worker runs.
%
But every region starts its threads afresh, and that time is charged
to scheduling, whether the region lasts a second or a millisecond.
%
And a program that makes many outermost calls, as a program written
over a library of parallel loops does, cannot be measured at all,
except by turning the whole program into one call, which puts the
loading of its input back into the region.

\paragraph{Alternative: a rendezvous}
%
A scheduler that keeps its workers, and whose recorders say nothing
about the stay in progress, must make every worker act at the
boundaries of a region: to zero its recorder at the start, and to
bring it up to date at the end.
%
TaskPaRTS has done this with a rendezvous built from
\lstinline|fork2join|~\cite{taskparts}.
%
A function forks, spins in its first branch until a flag is set, and
calls itself in its second.
%
A worker that spins cannot run the second branch, so another worker
must steal it, and after $P$ levels each worker holds one.
%
The last to arrive acts for all, sets the flag, and each worker then
acts for itself.
%
This works, but it is delicate.
%
It depends on how workers steal.
%
It costs $P$ steals at each boundary, just outside the region.
%
And the order of the shared action and the workers' own actions
matters: if the totals are added up before each worker has closed the
interval it is in, that interval is lost.
%
Snapshots need none of this, because a recorder keeps the start of
the stay in progress, and because an idle worker leaves its recorder
alone.

\section{What is left out}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Events at each fork}
%
A log entry at each fork, or a time for each task, would let a tool
rebuild the whole computation DAG.
%
But it would put a reading of the clock and a write to the log on the
path of every fork, which is the cost this paper avoids.
%
We leave it to a separate build for debugging.

\paragraph{Snapshots during a computation}
%
A snapshot is taken between outermost calls.
%
Reading the recorders while tasks run would need each worker to guard
its updates, for instance with a sequence number that a reader checks
before and after.
%
The updates happen in the loop, so this too would cost a fork
nothing, but we have not needed it.

\paragraph{Sleeping workers}
%
In micro taskparts, an idle worker spins, yielding its processor
between attempts to steal.
%
A scheduler that puts idle workers to sleep has a fourth state, which
it enters and leaves in its loop, so \lstinline|mark| records it in
the same way.
%
A sleeping worker writes nothing either, so a snapshot can treat it as
it treats an idle one.

\section{The ParlayLib examples as a whole}
\label{sec:suite}

Tables~\ref{tbl:suite} and~\ref{tbl:suitegamora} extend the
measurements of Appendix~\ref{sec:perf} from four programs to 60 of
the 63 example programs distributed with
ParlayLib~\cite{blelloch20parlaylib}, on the M5 and on the
Ryzen~\cite{flexibench-suite,flexibench-suitegamora}.
%
Table~\ref{tbl:suiteicelake} has them on the 80 cores of the Xeon.
%
Each program ran with three builds: no scheduler, micro taskparts
with no instrumentation, and micro taskparts with totals.
%
For each program, the tables give the time of each build and, from the
totals, the factors of Section~\ref{sec:what}.

\paragraph{Regions}
%
The drivers of these programs time their algorithm with ParlayLib's
timer.
%
In the build with totals, we changed the timer alone: it takes a
snapshot wherever it reads the clock, so the totals of a region are
those of the interval that the driver times.
%
The drivers and the algorithms are unmodified.

\paragraph{Inputs}
%
A program takes a size, or reads a file.
%
The graph programs read symmetric R-MAT
graphs~\cite{chakrabarti2004rmat} with about 20 edges per vertex, and
the text programs read texts of the Problem Based Benchmark
Suite~\cite{shun2012pbbs}.
%
The sizes and the files are those of the companion paper on native
fork join~\cite{nativeforkjoin}, which chose each size on the Ryzen:
it doubled the size until one run of the algorithm with no scheduler
took at least a second, or until the process used a quarter of the
machine's memory.
%
The M5 runs the same sizes.

\paragraph{Programs left out}
%
We left out three programs.
%
The drivers of hash\_map and decision\_tree\_c45 time several stages
in each repetition, which our analysis does not add up.
%
And we have no input for lasso\_regression.

\paragraph{Method}
%
Each program and build ran as three processes, interleaved, and a
process runs its algorithm up to 20 times.
%
A time or a total is the median over the three processes of the median
over a process's regions, and a factor is computed from these medians.
%
Flexibench checked the output of every process against the expected
output, for the worker counts of the tables and for two more, one of
them above the number of hardware threads.
%
Every check passed.

\paragraph{What the totals cost}
%
On one worker, the builds with and without totals are within 2\% of
each other on 54 of the 60 programs on the M5, and on 57 on the Ryzen.
%
On all cores, they are within 5\% of each other on 58 programs on each
machine.
%
The programs outside take 0.11~s or less there.

\paragraph{The price of the forks}
%
The median of $T_s/W_1$ is 0.96 on the M5 and 0.95 on the Ryzen, and
it is at least 0.9 on 49 and on 46 programs.
%
It is lowest for flatten and find\_if on the M5, at 0.46 and 0.54, and
for minimum\_edit\_distance and le\_list on the Ryzen, at 0.72 and
0.73.
%
A few programs run faster on one worker than with no scheduler, as
longest\_common\_prefix does on the Ryzen.

\paragraph{All cores}
%
The median speedup is 5.6 on the 10 cores of the M5, and 8.2 on the
16 cores of the Ryzen.
%
Most of the loss is work inflation, as for the four programs of
Appendix~\ref{sec:perf}: the median inflation is 1.66 and 1.71, and
the work at least doubles on 12 and on 16 programs.
%
It grows most where a program does little but read and write memory:
on the Ryzen, by 9.4 times for reduce and by 6.7 times for linefit.
%
Utilization is at least 0.95 on 49 programs on the M5 and on 43 on
the Ryzen.

\paragraph{Where the workers are idle}
%
On the M5, two programs stand apart, kcore and spectral\_separator.
%
Each takes about as long on all the cores as on four workers, and
they are the only programs whose utilization on all cores is below
0.8.
%
The totals show where the time goes: utilization on all cores is 0.53
and 0.57 on the M5, and 0.53 and 0.63 on the Ryzen.
%
On 32 workers of the Ryzen, a region of spectral\_separator holds
\fbval{sworkgamora}{spectral_separator}{P = 32}~s of work,
\fbval{sschedgamora}{spectral_separator}{P = 32}~s of scheduling and
\fbval{sidlegamora}{spectral_separator}{P = 32}~s of idle time.
%
So these programs often have work for only a few workers, and the
others spin (Appendix~\ref{sec:limits}).
%
On the Ryzen, utilization is also below 0.7 on all cores for flatten,
filter, scan, bigint\_add and cartesian\_tree.

\paragraph{All hardware threads}
%
From 16 workers to 32 on the Ryzen, the median speedup goes from 8.2
to 9.2, and the median inflation from 1.71 to 3.0.
%
The work at least doubles on 50 programs: two workers that share a
core each take longer over the same work.

\paragraph{Eighty cores}
%
Table~\ref{tbl:suiteicelake} has the same 60 programs on the
Xeon~\cite{flexibench-suiteicelake}, on 40 workers, one for each core
of a processor, on 80, and on 160, one for each hardware thread.
%
The sizes are those the companion paper uses on this machine, which
are larger than the Ryzen's for about a dozen programs.
%
The pages of every process are spread evenly over the two processors'
memories, and no thread is bound to a core.
%
The build with no scheduler and the runs on one worker ran as one
process each, not three, and the build with no instrumentation ran on
1 and on 80 workers only.

The totals stay free.
%
On one worker, the two builds are within 2\% of each other on 59
programs, and on 80 workers within 5\% on 58; the two programs outside
take 0.21~s or less there.
%
The median of $T_s/W_1$ is 0.95.

The median speedup is 30 on 40 workers and 48 on 80, and 40 programs
are at least 40 times faster on 80 workers than with no scheduler.
%
Four of them, karatsuba, knn, samplesort and triangle\_count, are 70
times faster or more.
%
Again the loss is mostly work inflation: its median goes from 1.19 on
40 workers to 1.44 on 80, and the work at least doubles on 6 and then
on 16 programs.
%
Utilization is at least 0.95 on 45 programs on 40 workers and on 30 on
80, and its median on 80 is 0.94.

The factors tell three kinds of slow program apart.
%
Flatten, scan and filter are no faster on 80 workers than on 40,
between 3.3 and 4.1 times faster than with no scheduler: their work grows by 11 to 13
times on 80 workers, and in step with the number of workers.
%
Each run of them writes to memory it has just been given, and most of
that growth is time the kernel spends handing out the pages, which a
worker's clock counts as work like any other.
%
Reduce and linefit do little but read and write memory: their work
grows by 4.6 and 3.7 times while their workers are never idle.
%
Kcore is the opposite: its work grows by
\fbval{sinflationicelake}{kcore}{P = 80} times, but its utilization
is \fbval{sutilicelake}{kcore}{P = 80}.
%
A region of it on 80 workers holds
\fbval{sworkicelake}{kcore}{P = 80}~s of work,
\fbval{sschedicelake}{kcore}{P = 80}~s of scheduling and
\fbval{sidleicelake}{kcore}{P = 80}~s of idle time.
%
And the forks of graph\_color cost more than anywhere else, with
$T_s/W_1$ at 0.76, though it is more than 42 times faster on 80 workers.

From 80 workers to 160, 40 programs get faster, knn the most, to 106
times.
%
The median speedup goes from 48 to 51, the median inflation from 1.44
to 2.5, and the median utilization from 0.94 to 0.90.

Figure~\ref{fig:factoredicelake} draws eight of the programs.
%
The four of Figure~\ref{fig:factored} lose more to work inflation
than to anything else, and quickhull and BFS a good deal more of it
than the two sorts.
%
The other four are the extremes of the table: knn, which loses little
to anything up to 80 workers; graph\_color, with the widest band for
the forks; kcore, where idle time takes more than inflation

A plot has four points only, at 1, 40, 80 and 160 workers, so it shows
