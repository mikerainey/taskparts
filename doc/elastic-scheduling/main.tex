\title{Elastic native fork join: workers that sleep, forks that do not pay}
\author{Mike Rainey}
\email{me@mike-rainey.site}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
A work-stealing scheduler whose idle workers keep looking for work
spends a processor on every worker that finds none.
%
\emph{Elastic scheduling} puts such workers to sleep, and wakes them
when work appears.
%
The difficulty is to do so without slowing down the fork, which under
native fork join costs a few nanoseconds, and without ever leaving a
worker asleep while a task waits to be stolen.
%
We present a small protocol that meets both goals.
%
One shared word counts the workers that are asleep and those that are
stealing.
%
Only idle workers write it; a fork reads it once, after its push, and
wakes a worker if some worker is asleep and none is stealing.
%
On x86-64, where that read would force a costly instruction into every
push, the worker about to sleep pays instead, through a service of the
operating system.
%
We state the invariant that rules out lost wake-ups, and measure what
the protocol costs and what it saves, in the terms of \emph{burn}: the
processor time a computation consumes.
%
On a program whose parallelism comes in bursts, the protocol removes
most of the burn, 96\% of it on 80 workers, at no cost in time.
%
On programs that speed up little it removes up to half, but that
saving is tied to how the operating system serves page faults: with
huge pages, the same programs run several times faster on 80 workers
and the protocol saves nothing.
%
On that machine the protocol costs about a tenth of the time of
programs that keep 80 workers busy.
%
A companion implementation adds the protocol to micro taskparts in
about 150 lines of C++.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Challenge: idle workers that cost nothing}

\paragraph{Burn}
%
Work stealing~\cite{blumofele99,arora98} keeps every worker busy: a
worker with nothing to do tries, again and again, to steal from the
others.
%
When the computation has work for every worker, almost every attempt
soon succeeds.
%
When it does not, the attempts are wasted, and each idle worker
occupies a processor for nothing.

We measure the waste as follows.
%
The \emph{burn} $B$ of a computation is the processor time it consumes
on all of its workers together, whether they work or look for work.
%
The burn is at least the work $T_1$, the time the computation takes on
one processor, so the \emph{burn ratio} $B / T_1$ is at least 1.
%
A scheduler whose $P$ workers never sleep has a burn of $P \cdot T_P$,
where $T_P$ is the time of the computation on $P$ workers.
%
So its burn ratio is close to 1 when the speedup is close to $P$, and
grows as large as $P$ when the computation is serial.
%
In our measurements, the burn is the user time plus the system time
that the operating system charges to the process: a worker that yields
its processor between attempts spends much of its time in the kernel.

Micro taskparts, the implementation of native fork
join~\cite{nativeforkjoin}, has such a scheduler: a worker that fails
to steal yields and tries again.
%
A program whose parallelism comes in bursts, or that speeds up
little for any other reason, then burns several times its work
(Section~\ref{sec:burn}).

\paragraph{Elastic scheduling}
%
An \emph{elastic} scheduler puts a worker to sleep when it finds no
work, and wakes one when work appears.
%
Three requirements make this harder than it sounds.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{The fork must not pay.}
  Under native fork join, a fork whose second branch is not stolen is
  a push, two calls, and a pop, and costs between 2 and
  5\,ns~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
  Work appears at a push, so the push must be able to wake a sleeper.
  But a write to shared memory at each push, or even one more atomic
  instruction, would show.
\item \emph{No wake-up may be lost.}
  A worker that decides to sleep just as another pushes a task must
  not sleep while that task waits.
\item \emph{The speedup must stay.}
  A program with work for every worker should run as fast as it does
  when no worker ever sleeps.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{This paper}
%
We extend native fork join with a protocol that meets the first two
requirements by construction.
%
The third it meets on a machine with 16 cores, within a few percent,
and misses on one with 80, where a program with work for 80 workers
takes about a tenth longer with the protocol.
%
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} presents the protocol, and
Section~\ref{sec:safety} the invariant that it maintains.
%
Section~\ref{sec:order} explains the one place where the protocol
touches the fork: the push and the read that follows it must take
effect in order, which is free on AArch64, and which on x86-64 we make
the sleeper pay for.
%
Section~\ref{sec:burn} measures time and burn on three machines,
against micro taskparts without the protocol and against the scheduler
of ParlayLib~\cite{blelloch20parlaylib}, which sleeps.
%
The protocol removes most of the burn of a program with bursts.
%
It also removes up to half of the burn of several programs that speed
up little, and there the saving goes with the page faults of a run
that fills new memory.
%
With huge pages those programs run several times faster on 80
workers, and the protocol saves nothing.
%
Section~\ref{sec:related} relates the protocol to waste-efficient work
stealing~\cite{singer2026wews}, a recent algorithm with proven bounds
on waste.

\section{The protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

We start from the algorithm of the native fork join
paper~\cite{nativeforkjoin}, and use its names.
%
Each worker runs a scheduler loop.
%
The loop counts the branches that reach a join, and otherwise calls
\lstinline|steal|, which tries to take the oldest task of another
worker, chosen at random.
%
\lstinline|fork2join| pushes a task for its second branch, runs its
first branch, and pops.
%
A worker's deque is empty whenever the worker is in its loop.

\paragraph{The status word}
%
The protocol adds one shared word, \lstinline|status|, which holds two
counts: the number of workers that are \emph{asleep}, and the number
that are \emph{stealing}.
%
A worker is stealing from the moment its loop starts looking for a
task until it has one, except while it is asleep.
%
A worker that is neither stealing nor asleep is \emph{working}: it
runs client code, or counts a branch.
%
Both counts fit in one machine word, so that one atomic instruction
can read both, or move a worker from one to the other.

We say that the computation \emph{needs a thief} when some worker is
asleep and none is stealing.
%
The whole protocol is this: \emph{whoever may have caused the need
for a thief checks for it, and if it holds, wakes one worker.}

\begin{figure}
\hrule
\begin{lstlisting}[escapechar=@]
status : atomic {asleep : int, stealing : int}
sleepers : semaphore

needs_thief(s) -> bool {
  return s.asleep > 0 && s.stealing == 0 }

ensure_thief() -> void {
  atomically {
    if (!needs_thief(status)) return
    status.asleep -= 1; status.stealing += 1 }
  post(sleepers) }

sleep() -> void {  // the caller is stealing
  atomically {  // announce
    status.stealing -= 1; status.asleep += 1 }
  if (done || some deque holds a task) {  // look
    atomically {
      if (status.asleep > 0) {  // take it back
        status.asleep -= 1; status.stealing += 1
        return } } }
  wait(sleepers) }  // woken: stealing again

acquire() -> task* {  // replaces steal in the loop
  atomically { status.stealing += 1 }
  failed = 0; t = null
  while (!done) {
    t = steal(); if (t != null) break
    failed += 1
    if (failed == patience) { failed = 0; sleep() } }
  s = atomically { status.stealing -= 1; status }
  if (t != null && needs_thief(s)) ensure_thief()
  return t }

fork2join($\fo$, $\ft$ : () -> void) -> void {
  j = join{pending = 2}
  t = task{body = $\ft$, j = &j}
  push(&t)@\stepc{1}@
  if (needs_thief(status)) ensure_thief()@\stepc{1$'$}@
  $\fo$()
  ...  // steps 2 to 4, unchanged
\end{lstlisting}
\hrule
\caption{Elastic scheduling for native fork join.  Each
  \lstinline|atomically| block is one atomic instruction on the status
  word, or a loop around a compare-and-swap.}
\label{fig:elastic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Going to sleep}
%
In the scheduler loop, \lstinline|acquire| replaces
\lstinline|steal| (Figure~\ref{fig:elastic}).
%
It counts its worker as stealing, and tries to steal until it
succeeds.
%
After \lstinline|patience| failures in a row, it calls
\lstinline|sleep|.

\lstinline|sleep| works in two steps, and their order matters.
%
It first \emph{announces}: in one atomic step, it moves its worker from
the stealing count to the asleep count.
%
It then \emph{looks} at every deque.
%
If one of them holds a task, it takes the announcement back and
returns, and \lstinline|acquire| goes on stealing.
%
Otherwise, it waits on a semaphore that all sleepers share.

\paragraph{Waking up}
%
\lstinline|ensure_thief| checks, in one atomic step, whether the
computation needs a thief, and if so moves one worker from the asleep
count to the stealing count.
%
It then posts the semaphore, which lets one sleeper return from
\lstinline|sleep|.
%
The waker moves the count on the sleeper's behalf, before the sleeper
has run a single instruction.
%
So from that moment the computation no longer needs a thief, and a
second waker that checks finds nothing to do: one need wakes one
worker.

A sleeper that has announced, seen a task, and is about to take its
announcement back may find that a waker has already claimed it.
%
The asleep count is then zero.
%
The sleeper waits on the semaphore anyway, where the waker's post
releases it at once.

\paragraph{Who checks}
%
Two events can make the computation need a thief while a task waits.
%
The first is a push, which makes a task appear: so
\lstinline|fork2join| reads the status word after its push
(step~1$'$), and calls \lstinline|ensure_thief| if a thief is needed.
%
This read is all that the protocol adds to a fork.
%
The second is the departure of the last thief.
%
A thief that goes to sleep looks at the deques first, as we saw.
%
A thief that succeeds takes one task, and its victim may hold more:
so \lstinline|acquire| checks as it leaves, using the value of the
status word that its own decrement returned.

\paragraph{The end}
%
When the root task finishes, \lstinline|finish| sets
\lstinline|done|, and then wakes every sleeper, one
\lstinline|ensure_thief|-like step at a time.
%
A woken worker finds \lstinline|done| set, and its loop exits.

\paragraph{What does not change}
%
The deque is used as before, plus a query that tells a sleeper whether
it holds a task.
%
Steps 2 to 4 of \lstinline|fork2join|, the joins, and the context
switches are untouched.
%
In particular, a worker that suspends a parent at a join returns to its
loop, and may then go to sleep, even though the parent is not finished.
%
This is as it should be: whichever worker counts the last branch
resumes the parent, and that worker is awake.

\section{Why no worker sleeps on work}
\label{sec:safety}

The protocol maintains the following invariant.

\begin{quote}
\emph{If a worker is asleep and a deque holds a task, then some worker
is stealing, or some worker is between an event that may have ended
this and the check that follows it.}
\end{quote}

A worker that is stealing keeps choosing victims at random until it
succeeds, so a task that waits is found, unless its owner pops it
first.
%
The invariant rests on four facts.

\paragraph{A sleeper and a fork cannot miss each other}
%
The sleeper writes the status word, then reads the deques.
%
The fork writes its deque, then reads the status word.
%
Provided each one's write takes effect before its own read
(Section~\ref{sec:order}), one of the two reads comes after the other
worker's write.
%
Either the sleeper sees the task, and does not sleep, or the fork sees
the sleeper, and calls \lstinline|ensure_thief|.
%
The sleeper reads the deques one after another, but the argument holds
for each deque separately, because the announcement precedes all of
those reads.

\paragraph{The last thief does not leave unnoticed}
%
The stealing count reaches zero in two ways.
%
A thief announces that it sleeps: it then looks at every deque, and
stays if one holds a task.
%
Or a thief leaves with a task: the decrement that removes it from the
count returns the status word as that same atomic step left it, and if
it shows a need, the thief calls \lstinline|ensure_thief|.
%
A worker that falls asleep after that decrement looks at the deques
itself.

\paragraph{Counts and tokens agree}
%
A waker posts the semaphore exactly once for each worker it moves out
of the asleep count, and a worker waits on the semaphore exactly when
it has not moved itself out.
%
A compare-and-swap on the status word decides each race between a
waker and a sleeper that takes its announcement back.
%
So no post is left over, which would let a later sleeper through with
no task to steal, and no sleeper waits for a post that is not coming.

\paragraph{The end reaches every worker}
%
\lstinline|finish| sets \lstinline|done| before it wakes the sleepers.
%
A worker that announces afterwards reads \lstinline|done| when it
looks, and takes its announcement back.

\paragraph{What is not guaranteed}
%
The invariant says that a waiting task has a thief looking for it.
%
It bounds neither how long the thief takes to find the task nor how
many workers are awake.
%
A burst of work wakes its workers one at a time: each push or steal
that finds a need wakes one worker, which steals, and whose own
departure or pushes wake the next.
%
We have not proved a bound on running time or on burn;
Section~\ref{sec:related} points to an algorithm that has both.

\section{Ordering a push and a read}
\label{sec:order}

The first fact of Section~\ref{sec:safety} needs the store of a push
to take effect before the read of the status word that follows it.
%
Processors do not promise this by default.
%
A store waits in a buffer of its processor before it reaches memory,
and a later load of another location can be served first.
%
If both the fork and the sleeper run ahead of their own stores, each
reads the old value of the other's location: the fork sees no sleeper,
the sleeper sees no task, and the wake-up is lost.

\paragraph{The direct way}
%
In C++, a sequentially consistent store followed by a sequentially
consistent load are ordered as needed.
%
The push of micro taskparts ends by storing the new bottom index of the
deque, with release ordering, which is enough to publish the task to
thieves~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
%
Making that one store sequentially consistent is the whole change.
%
On AArch64 it costs nothing: compilers emit the same instruction for
both orderings, and the load that follows is ordered after
it.
%
On the M5, \lstinline|fib(38)|, which does little but fork, takes
\fibtime{elastic}{1}\,s on one worker with the protocol and
\fibtime{plain}{1}\,s without~\cite{flexibench-elastic-fib}.

On x86-64, a sequentially consistent store is a locked instruction.
%
On the Ryzen, \lstinline|fib(38)| on one worker takes
\fibtimeg{ordered}{1}\,s with such a push, against
\fibtimeg{plain}{1}\,s without the protocol
(Table~\ref{tbl:fib})~\cite{flexibench-elastic-fib-gamora}: every
fork pays about 3\,ns, more than half of what a fork cost before.
%
On the Xeon, a machine with two Intel Xeon Platinum 8380 processors of
40 cores each, \lstinline|fib(45)| on one worker takes
\fibtimex{ordered}{1}\,s with such a push, against
\fibtimex{plain}{1}\,s without the
protocol~\cite{flexibench-elastic-fib-icelake}, 71\% more.
%
This breaks the first requirement.

\paragraph{Making the sleeper pay}
%
The race has two sides, and only one of them is in a hurry.
%
A fork runs millions of times a second; a worker goes to sleep after
\lstinline|patience| failed steals, and then stops for a long time.
%
So we leave the push as it was, and let the sleeper order the stores
of everyone else.
%
Linux provides the means: the system call
\lstinline|membarrier|, with the command for private expedited
barriers, returns once every running thread of the process has
executed a memory barrier~\cite{membarrier}.
%
A thread that is not running has no store in flight.

On x86-64 Linux, \lstinline|sleep| makes this call between its
announcement and its look at the deques.
%
Consider a fork whose read of the status word came before the
announcement took effect, so that it saw no sleeper.
%
Its push precedes that read in its program, so when the barrier runs
on its thread, the push has been executed, and the barrier sends its
store to memory.
%
The sleeper then sees the task.
%
A fork whose read comes after the announcement sees the sleeper.
%
Either way, the first fact of Section~\ref{sec:safety} holds, and the
fork executes no instruction that it did not execute before, except
the read.

With this change, \lstinline|fib(38)| on the Ryzen takes
\fibtimeg{elastic}{1}\,s on one worker and
\fibtimeg{elastic}{16}\,s on 16, the same as without the protocol
(Table~\ref{tbl:fib}).
%
On the Xeon, \lstinline|fib(45)| takes \fibtimex{elastic}{1}\,s on one
worker, within 1\% of its time without the protocol.
%
On more workers the protocol costs time there:
\fibtimex{elastic}{40}\,s against \fibtimex{plain}{40}\,s on 40
workers, 8\% more, and \fibtimex{elastic}{160}\,s against
\fibtimex{plain}{160}\,s on 160, 10\% more.
%
On 80 workers, where the run lasts a third of a second, the gap is
wider, \fibtimex{elastic}{80}\,s against \fibtimex{plain}{80}\,s, a
third more; we have not established its cause.

The price moves to the sleeper: one system call per attempt to sleep,
which interrupts the workers that are running.
%
Section~\ref{sec:burn} measures programs in which workers sleep often.

Micro taskparts uses the remote barrier on x86-64 Linux, and the
sequentially consistent store elsewhere.
%
On x86-64 without such a system call, the choice is between paying at
each fork and giving up the guarantee.

\paragraph{Programs}
%
We measured six programs with three schedulers: micro taskparts as the
native fork join paper leaves it, which never sleeps, micro taskparts
with the protocol, and the scheduler of ParlayLib.
%
Four of the programs are the examples of ParlayLib that the native
fork join paper measures~\cite{nativeforkjoin}: two sorts, a convex
hull, and a breadth-first search.
%
They have work for every worker most of the time.
%
The fifth, \emph{bursty}, is ours.
%
It alternates two phases ten times: \lstinline|fib(38)| computed with
no parallelism, and the same computed with forks down to
\lstinline|fib(20)|.
%
So half of its work is serial, and the other half arrives in bursts
of a few milliseconds on many workers.
%
It is written against ParlayLib, like the examples, so that the same
source runs on all three schedulers.
%
The sixth is \lstinline|fib(38)| on micro taskparts alone, which
Section~\ref{sec:order} used.

Each program runs its algorithm three or five times in one process,
and records the elapsed time of each run, and the user and system time
of the process over that run.
%
Each program and scheduler ran as five processes, interleaved.
%
An elapsed time in the tables is the median over the processes of the
median over a process's runs; a burn is the sum of the user time and
of the system time obtained in the same way.
%
Flexibench recorded the runs, checked the result of every one, and
certified the
values~\cite{flexibench-elastic-parlay,flexibench-elastic-parlay-gamora}.
%
The M5 has 4 fast and 6 slower cores, and the Ryzen 16 cores, all
alike, with two hardware threads each.

On the Xeon, which has 80 cores on two processors and two hardware
threads per core, we measured nine programs, on larger inputs.
%
They are the five above; bursty with \lstinline|fib(42)| in place of
\lstinline|fib(38)|, whose phases last seven times as long; and three
more examples of ParlayLib, which no scheduler we measured speeds up
by a factor of 12 on 80 workers: a prefix sum (scan), an addition of
two large integers, and a $k$-core decomposition of a graph.
%
There each program and scheduler ran as three processes, with its
memory interleaved over the two processors and no worker pinned to a
core~\cite{flexibench-elastic-stacks-icelake}.

A stolen task runs on a stack of its own, which its thief takes from a
store of stacks and returns at the join.
%
In every micro taskparts that we measured, each worker keeps a few such
stacks for itself, as in the native fork join
paper~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
%
The Xeon's round also measured a version whose workers share one pool
of stacks under a lock, and on one program the pool decides the
result: kcore, which steals often, takes
\etimek{kcore}{plain 80}\,s on 80 workers with the pool and no
protocol, and \etimek{kcore}{plain2 80}\,s without the pool.

\paragraph{Patience}
%
Table~\ref{tbl:patience} varies \lstinline|patience| on the
Xeon~\cite{flexibench-elastic-patience2-icelake}.

A worker that waits a hundred times longer before it sleeps burns

No value is best in every row: 100 gives the least burn on 160

The value 1000 is within 30\% of the least in each row.

%
The other four programs show what the protocol costs when there is
little to save.
%
On 16 workers of the Ryzen, each takes with the protocol within 1\% of
the time it takes without, and burns within 2\% of what it burns
without.
%
On the M5 the times are less steady: with the protocol, a program
takes between 14\% less and 9\% more than without, and the three

%
On 32 workers the search is faster with the protocol,
\etimeg{BFS}{elastic 32}\,s against \etimeg{BFS}{plain 32}\,s, and the

ParlayLib's scheduler is within 11\% of ours on every program, faster

On 80 workers of the Xeon, where these four programs run for 30 to

the search, 10\% on the hull, and 17\% on samplesort, the shortest
run.

ParlayLib's scheduler takes the same time as ours on the search, and

\paragraph{Programs that do not scale}
%
The last three programs of Table~\ref{tbl:burnx} speed up little on
80 workers, and the scheduler that never sleeps burns many times
their work.
%
On scan and bigint\_add, the protocol removes over 40\% of that burn,
and the programs take no longer with it.
%
ParlayLib's scheduler does better still on these two: it is faster
than ours by about 12\%, and burns about a fifth less.
%
Most of the burn of these two programs is system time under every
scheduler: of scan's burn with the protocol,
\fbval{ksysicelake}{scan}{elastic2 80}\,s is spent in the kernel, and
with no scheduler at all, \fbval{ksysicelake}{scan}{serial}\,s of
\etimek{scan}{serial}\,s.
%
Each run fills memory that it has just obtained, and what limits these
programs is the kernel's handling of the page faults, not a scheduler.
%
We ran scan, bigint\_add and two programs like them again with the
kernel set to back all memory with huge pages, which a run obtains
with far fewer faults~\cite{flexibench-elastic-thp-icelake}.
%
Scan then takes \fbval{htimeicelake}{scan}{plain 80}\,s on 80 workers
with the scheduler that never sleeps, a fifth of its time before, and
burns \hburn{scan}{plain 80}\,s.
%
The protocol then saves nothing: with it scan takes
\fbval{htimeicelake}{scan}{elastic 80}\,s and burns
\hburn{scan}{elastic 80}\,s, and each of the four programs takes
between 11\% and 23\% longer than without it, the cost that the
programs with work for every worker show.
%
ParlayLib's scheduler, the fastest of the three on these programs
before, is then the slowest, at
\fbval{htimeicelake}{scan}{homegrown 80}\,s on scan.
%
So the burn that the protocol removes from these programs goes with
the page faults: it is there to remove under the setting of the other
rounds, in which a program gets huge pages only if it asks, and not
under this one.
%
On kcore the protocol costs time, \etimek{kcore}{elastic2 80}\,s
against \etimek{kcore}{plain2 80}\,s, 11\% more, and saves a third of
the burn, \burnk{kcore}{elastic2 80}\,s against
\burnk{kcore}{plain2 80}\,s.
%
ParlayLib's scheduler is faster there, \etimek{kcore}{homegrown 80}\,s,
and burns more than ours, \burnk{kcore}{homegrown 80}\,s.

On one worker of the Ryzen, where nothing ever sleeps, the protocol
changes the time of none of the four by as much as 1\%, nor that of
\lstinline|fib(38)| (Table~\ref{tbl:fib}).

\paragraph{What these runs do not show}
%
The burn of a program with work for every worker is nearly its work
plus the inflation of that work on a loaded machine, and the protocol
has little to remove.
%
So the tables bound the cost of the protocol on such programs, and
show its benefit on one program with bursts, of one shape and two
sizes.
%
How the benefit varies with the length of the bursts and of the gaps
between them, beyond those two sizes, we have not measured.

\section{Related work and limits}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Waste-efficient work stealing}
%
The algorithm of Singer, Agrawal, and
Schardl~\cite{singer2026wews} is a work stealer that sleeps, with
proven bounds, in expectation:
\[
\text{time}\;\; T_1/P + O(T_\infty), \qquad
\text{waste}\;\; O(\min\{P\,T_\infty,\; T_1 + P^2\}),
\]
where the waste is the processor time not spent on work, that is, the
burn minus the work.
%
Their algorithm keeps an account of the time workers spend working and
the time they spend stealing, puts a thief to sleep when stealing has
run far enough ahead, and wakes one when work has.
%
In their implementation, in OpenCilk, one thief at a time gathers the
accounts of all workers and decides how many to put to sleep or to
wake.
%
A worker that spawns does nothing for the protocol.

Our protocol is of the other kind, which they call not
\emph{work-first}: a worker that makes work may have to wake another.
%
They measure such a protocol as a baseline, modeled on those of
ParlayLib and Rayon, and report that it slows down \lstinline|fib|
on one worker, by the overhead it adds to each spawn.
%
Section~\ref{sec:order} locates such a cost, for our protocol, in the
ordering of the push and the read, and removes it: what remains at a
fork is one read of a word that working workers never write.
%
What our protocol lacks is their analysis.
%
It has no account of time, so nothing bounds its burn when work
appears and disappears faster than workers can profitably wake and
sleep, and it wakes workers one at a time, where their algorithm
wakes as many as the account calls for.

\paragraph{ParlayLib's scheduler}
%
The scheduler of ParlayLib~\cite{blelloch20parlaylib} also wakes at a
push.
%
Its push reports whether the deque was empty, and if so, and if some
worker is asleep, it wakes one; a thief does the same when the deque
it steals from holds more.
%
A worker sleeps once it has failed to steal for 10 milliseconds.
%
Its push stores the bottom index of the deque with sequentially
consistent ordering, so on x86-64 it pays at every fork the cost that
Section~\ref{sec:order} measures.

\paragraph{Limits}
%
The measurements of Section~\ref{sec:burn} cover three machines and
nine programs, and those of Appendix~\ref{sec:collection} two machines
and 21; one program, written for the purpose, has bursts.
%
On the Xeon the protocol costs about a tenth of the time of most
programs that keep 80 workers busy, and we have not established where
that time goes.
%
The Xeon's runs on 80 workers were free to use any of its 160
hardware threads.
%
Restricted to one hardware thread per core, ParlayLib's scheduler is
at least 10\% faster on most of 60 programs and the scheduler that
never sleeps is unchanged~\cite{flexibench-suitepercore}, so the
differences we report there between ParlayLib's scheduler and ours
depend on where the kernel places the workers; we have not run the
protocol under that restriction.
%
We have varied \lstinline|patience| on one machine and on that program
alone.
%
We have not measured the protocol when other processes compete for the
processors, which is where idle workers that spin do the most harm.
%
And we have proved nothing about time or burn.

\paragraph{Implementation}
%
The micro taskparts of this paper is in the directory
\path{doc/elastic-scheduling/microtaskparts} of the TaskPaRTS
repository~\cite{taskparts}.
%
It is the micro taskparts of the native fork join paper plus the
protocol: a section of about 150 lines, comments included, and a few
lines elsewhere, in the push, the loop, \lstinline|finish|, the code
that starts the workers, and \lstinline|fork2join|.

\section{Instrumentation}
\label{sec:instr}

Burn says how much processor time a run consumed, not where it went.
%
To take a burn apart, the micro taskparts of this paper can be built
with the instrumentation of the instrumentation
paper~\cite{instrumentation}, extended for workers that sleep.
%
It is off unless a build turns it on.
%
A build without it has the instructions it had before, except in the
function that wakes a worker, which is out of line and now returns
whether it woke one.

\paragraph{What carries over}
%
Each worker has a \emph{recorder}, which only that worker writes: the
state the worker is in, the time at which it entered that state, the
total time it has spent in each state, and a few counts.
%
The scheduler loop is the only code that changes a worker's state, so
it is the only code that reads the clock: a fork that is not stolen
records nothing.
%
The recorder sits after the deque in the worker, on cache lines of
its own.
%
The states of that paper are \emph{working}, in client code;
\emph{scheduling}, in the loop with something to do; and \emph{idle},
in the loop and failing to steal.

\paragraph{A fourth state}
%
A worker that waits for a token is \emph{asleep}.
%
It enters the state just before the wait of \lstinline|sleep|, and is
idle again when the wait returns; the announcement and the look at the
deques that precede the wait count as idle time.
%
With $Z_P$ the total time asleep,
\[ P \cdot T_P = W_P + S_P + I_P + Z_P, \]
and the burn of the run is close to $W_P + S_P + I_P$, since a worker
that is asleep uses no processor.
%
The protocol's purpose, in these terms, is to turn idle time into time
asleep, and the three terms say what a burn above the work is made of:
work that takes longer on a loaded machine, the scheduler's own
operations, or thieves that are awake and find nothing.

\paragraph{Counts}
%
A recorder counts the steals of its worker, and four events of the
protocol: the times the worker went to sleep; the times it announced
that it would and took the announcement back, because a task had
appeared; the workers it woke at a fork; and the workers it woke after
a steal.
%
The third of these is the only one counted in client code, and it is
counted in the branch of \lstinline|fork2join| that has already found
a worker to wake.

\paragraph{Measuring a region}
%
A program measures a region of its execution by taking a
\emph{snapshot} before it and another after it: a snapshot is the sum
of the recorders at one instant, and the totals of a region are the
difference of two.
%
Recorders are never reset.
%
In the instrumentation paper, the workers outlive a computation, and a
snapshot is taken between computations, once every other worker is
idle.
%
Here the workers live for one call to \lstinline|launch|, and a
ParlayLib program runs its whole \lstinline|main| inside one such
call.
%
So a snapshot is taken inside the computation, by the root task, at a
point where it has no fork in progress: between two runs of a
benchmark's algorithm, for instance.
%
At such a point no task exists, so every other worker goes to sleep
within \lstinline|patience| failed steals, and no fork and no thief
posts a token.
%
The snapshot waits until the status word shows every other worker
asleep and none stealing, then until each recorder says that its
worker has reached its wait, and reads the recorders.
%
None of them can change before the root task forks again.

The wait has a price that the instrumentation paper's does not: every
region begins with all workers but one asleep, and ends only after the
others have used up their patience.
%
The first is also true of an uninstrumented run that follows
sequential code.
%
The second adds to a region's idle time up to \lstinline|patience|
failed steals for each worker.

\paragraph{A factored speedup with sleep}
%
The instrumentation paper draws a speedup as what is left of $P$ after
four losses: to the forks, to work inflation, to idle time and to
scheduling.
%
With $T_s$ the time of the program with no scheduler, the first three
curves are $P \cdot T_s / W_1$, $P \cdot T_s / W_P$ and
$P \cdot T_s / (W_P + I_P)$.
%
We add a fourth, $P \cdot T_s / (W_P + I_P + Z_P)$, below them: the
band above it is the speedup lost to sleep, and the band below it,
down to the speedup $T_s / T_P$, is the loss to scheduling.
%
The loss to sleep is the one loss that costs no burn.

Figure~\ref{fig:factored} has the plots of eight programs of the
collection of Appendix~\ref{sec:collection}, and
Table~\ref{tbl:counts} their counts on the Ryzen.
%
They are of four kinds.

\emph{Bursts.}
%
What bursty loses, it loses to sleep: on 32 workers, the workers are
asleep for \ftot{asleep}{bursty}\,s in total and idle for
\ftot{idle}{bursty}\,s, against \ftot{work}{bursty}\,s of work.

\emph{Stretches with one task.}
%
Scan sleeps too, with no burst written in it: on 32 workers its
workers sleep \ftot{asleep}{scan}\,s against
\ftot{work}{scan}\,s of work, and are idle for
\ftot{idle}{scan}\,s.
%
Its workers found nothing to steal for about two fifths of the run,
and slept through it.
%
Flatten, filter, bigint\_add and 2d\_linear\_program are alike: their
workers are asleep for between a quarter and a half of the run.
%
Table~\ref{tbl:counts} says how seldom that happens: scan's
workers go to sleep \ftot{sleeps}{scan} times in a run, once each,
which is the least a measured run can have, since the snapshot that
ends it waits for them to sleep.
%
So each worker but the first sleeps through one long stretch, and the
work of that stretch is one task's.
%
We have not established what the task does there.
%
These are the programs from which the protocol removes a third to a
half of the burn, and it makes each of them faster on 32 workers
(Table~\ref{tbl:suite}), which is what we expect when a thief that
sleeps leaves the other hardware thread of its core to a worker that
has a task.

\emph{Inflation.}
%
Reduce loses nearly everything to work inflation, as scan does above
its sleep: its work grows from \fbval{fworkgamora}{reduce}{P = 1}\,s
on one worker to \ftot{work}{reduce}\,s on 32.
%
No worker is idle or asleep for long, and no scheduler can give the
time back.
%
Work, in these totals, is all the time a worker spends outside the
scheduler loop: it includes what the kernel does on the worker's
behalf, such as handling its page faults.

\emph{Thieves that are never out of work for long.}
%
Kcore and spectral\_separator lose much to idle time, with the
protocol on: on 32 workers kcore's workers are idle for
\ftot{idle}{kcore}\,s, more than their \ftot{work}{kcore}\,s of work,
and asleep for \ftot{asleep}{kcore}\,s.
%
They steal \ftot{steals}{kcore} times in a run and go to sleep
\ftot{sleeps}{kcore} times.
%
The program makes many small tasks, a few at a time: a thief fails
often, and succeeds before it has failed \lstinline|patience| times
in a row, so it seldom sleeps.
%
The protocol removes a third of the burn of kcore
(Table~\ref{tbl:suite}) and leaves this idle time in place.
%
A smaller patience would trade it for more sleeps and more wakes; we
have not measured that trade on these programs.

\emph{Work for every worker.}
%
On samplesort, as on seven other programs of the collection, the
workers are idle or asleep for under a tenth of the run, and on all
but one of the eight they go to sleep only for the snapshot.
%
The forks of such a run wake few workers: the first fork wakes one,
and each thief that succeeds wakes the next
(Table~\ref{tbl:counts}).
%
After that, a fork reads the status word, finds no worker asleep, and
goes on.

\paragraph{On the Xeon}
%
Figure~\ref{fig:factoredx} has the same eight programs on the Xeon, on
larger inputs, and the right half of Table~\ref{tbl:counts} their
counts on 80 workers.
%
The kinds are the same, and larger.
%
Scan's workers are asleep for \ftotx{asleep}{scan}\,s on 80 workers,
against \ftotx{work}{scan}\,s of work, and still go to sleep once
each.
%
Reduce does \fbval{fworkicelake}{reduce}{P = 1}\,s of work on one
worker and \ftotx{work}{reduce}\,s on 80: its speedup stays under 18,
and nearly all of the loss is work inflation.
%
Two things differ.
%
Kcore's workers do sleep here, \ftotx{sleeps}{kcore} times in a run,
for \ftotx{asleep}{kcore}\,s in total, about as long as they work,
\ftotx{work}{kcore}\,s; they are idle for \ftotx{idle}{kcore}\,s more.
%
And scheduling, which is too small to see on the Ryzen, takes
\ftotx{sched}{spectral_separator}\,s in total of
spectral\_separator's run on 80 workers, beside \ftotx{idle}{spectral_separator}\,s of idle time
and \ftotx{work}{spectral_separator}\,s of work: its workers steal
\ftotx{steals}{spectral_separator} times.

\paragraph{What the protocol costs}
%
With the protocol, no program of the collection takes over 6\% longer
than without it.
%
The largest difference is on graph\_color,
\fbval{stimegamora}{graph_color}{elastic 32}\,s against
\fbval{stimegamora}{graph_color}{plain 32}\,s; le\_list and
minimum\_edit\_distance take about 3\% and 2\% longer, and eleven
programs are within 2\% either way.
%
The other seven are faster with it.

\paragraph{What it saves}
%
The protocol removes 94\% of the burn of bursty, about half of the
burn of scan, flatten and filter, and about a third of that of
bigint\_add, 2d\_linear\_program and kcore.
%
Flatten, filter and scan also run about 10\% faster with it,
bigint\_add 7\%, and bursty 25\%.
%
It removes between 5\% and 16\% of the burn of seven more programs,
down to minimum\_edit\_distance in the table, and changes that of the
last seven by 3\% or less.
%
Appendix~\ref{sec:instr} says where the removed burn was: in workers
that had nothing to steal for a long stretch.

\paragraph{The two schedulers that sleep}
%
On 13 of the 21 programs, ParlayLib's scheduler and ours are within
5\% of each other in time.
%
Ours is faster on kcore, where ParlayLib's takes
\stimeg{kcore}{homegrown 32}\,s against \stimeg{kcore}{elastic 32}\,s
and burns \sburng{kcore}{homegrown 32}\,s against
\sburng{kcore}{elastic 32}\,s, and on delaunay and quicksort, where
it takes about twice and 1.6 times as long.
%
On the last two the scheduler that never sleeps is as fast as ours,
so what separates them is not the protocol.
%
Ours is also faster by 7\% to 9\% on set\_cover,
minimum\_edit\_distance and bursty.
%
ParlayLib's is faster by 9\% on spectral\_separator and on
graph\_color, and burns 7\% less on the first.
%
On bursty it burns 60\% more than ours, as in
Table~\ref{tbl:burng}.

\paragraph{On the Xeon}
%
Table~\ref{tbl:suitex} has the same programs on 80 workers of the
Xeon, at the sizes the native fork join paper uses on that
machine~\cite{nativeforkjoin}.
%
Each scheduler ran on 40, 80 and 160 workers, as three processes each,
with memory interleaved over the two processors and no worker
pinned~\cite{flexibench-elastic-suite-icelake}.

The protocol saves what it saves on the Ryzen.
%
On 80 workers it removes 96\% of the burn of bursty, between 45\% and
50\% of that of scan, flatten and filter, 39\% of that of bigint\_add
and a quarter of that of kcore.
%
On 160 workers, two to a core, it removes about half of the burn of
each of the last five, and scan, flatten, filter and bigint\_add also
run about 20\% faster with it.
%
For those four the saving depends on how the kernel backs memory:
with huge pages, on 80 workers, they run four to six times faster and
the protocol saves no burn (Section~\ref{sec:burn}).

It costs more than on the Ryzen.
%
On 80 workers, fourteen programs take between 8\% and 19\% longer
with the protocol, and minimum\_edit\_distance 31\% longer,
\stimex{minimum_edit_distance}{elastic 80}\,s against
\stimex{minimum_edit_distance}{plain 80}\,s.
%
The burn of each of them but kcore grows too.
%
On minimum\_edit\_distance the workers never sleep in the course of a
run: they go to sleep \ftotx{sleeps}{minimum_edit_distance} times,
once each, for the snapshot that ends it
(Appendix~\ref{sec:instr}).
%
So the time is not spent going to sleep or waking; it is the cost that
\lstinline|fib(45)| shows on 80 workers in
Section~\ref{sec:order}, and we have not established where it goes.
%
It is smaller with fewer workers and with more: on 40 workers, three
programs take 8\% to 11\% longer with the protocol and the others at
most 6\%, and on 160, graph\_color and minimum\_edit\_distance take
19\% longer and the others at most 7\%.

ParlayLib's scheduler and ours differ more here than on the Ryzen.
%
On 80 workers, ParlayLib's is faster by 11\% to 19\% on scan,
flatten, filter, bigint\_add and kcore, by a quarter on push\_relabel,
and takes about half as long on spectral\_separator,
\stimex{spectral_separator}{homegrown 80}\,s against
\stimex{spectral_separator}{elastic 80}\,s.
%
Ours is faster on eight programs: ParlayLib's takes 11\% to 39\% longer
on six of them, and about 1.8 and 2.3 times as long on quicksort and
delaunay.
%
On bursty ParlayLib's burns \sburnx{bursty}{homegrown 80}\,s against
\sburnx{bursty}{elastic 80}\,s, 2.6 times as much.
